Chapter IV — The Exit

Rails built outside the system

Chapters I–III described the fiat machine: managed by experts, plumbed through banks, with a trap in the legal basement. This chapter is the parallel stack, engineered on the opposite premises. Read it bottom-up: the bedrock first, then the two rooms built on top of it. Every element carries a note on how the fiat world does the same job — because the differences are the whole point.

Orientation

The stack, bottom to top

One foundation, two rooms. Bitcoin's base layer settles ~7 transactions per second, globally, with every transaction verified by thousands of independent nodes — maximally trustworthy, deliberately slow. The layers above trade a measured amount of trust for speed, privacy, and usability, while the base layer remains the final arbiter of truth underneath everything.

Meanwhile, in the fiat world

The fiat stack runs the other way up: the "bedrock" is a committee. The Fed sets rules at the top, banks build on the Fed, and you sit at the bottom holding IOUs — with no layer you can verify and no floor you can exit to. This stack puts the unchangeable part at the bottom and the trust at the top, where you can see it.

Layer 1

The bedrock — five properties

Every Layer 2 inherits its security from these five. Understanding why the base layer has each property explains both why the L2s exist and what they sacrifice.

1.Proof of work — history costs energy

Miners expend real electricity to propose blocks; rewriting history means redoing all that work. No block producer can be cheaply bribed or threatened, and when an L2 "pegs" to the base layer, its final settlement is protected by the full hash rate of the network.

Meanwhile, in the fiat world

The ledger is secured by legal authority, not energy — so rewriting it is a policy decision. Bail-ins, account freezes, and the collateral sweep from Chapter III are all "valid transactions" there.

2.The UTXO model — coins with a lineage

Bitcoin doesn't track account balances; it tracks unspent outputs, each with a traceable history. That makes pegs precise: lock a UTXO on L1, receive L2 credit; burn the credit, unlock the UTXO. Ownership is a fact on the chain, not an entry in someone else's book.

Meanwhile, in the fiat world

You don't hold money or securities — you hold claims on intermediaries: bank IOUs, "security entitlements" in street name. Chapter III walked that chain of claims. Here the chain of custody is one link long: you.

3.Full nodes — rules by consensus

Roughly 15,000 public nodes independently verify every rule on every transaction. The rule set can't change without broad consensus — which is why L2s can rely on the peg: the rules guaranteeing your locked coin can't be altered by any single actor.

Meanwhile, in the fiat world

The rules are set by committee and can change overnight — a rate decision, an emergency facility, a UCC revision. Chapter I explained who trained the committee. Here, there is no committee.

4.Ten-minute blocks — slow, then final

A block every ~10 minutes; irreversible settlement after ~6 blocks (~60 minutes). Too slow for coffee — and that's the honest price of global finality. It's the main reason L2s exist: fast "local" finality on top, with disputes ultimately settled on the base layer's slower clock.

Meanwhile, in the fiat world

Payments feel instant but settle days later through ACH, Fedwire, and the DTCC — and stay reversible and freezable throughout. Fiat is fast provisionally and never quite final; Bitcoin is slow provisionally and then absolutely final.

5.Script & Taproot — the toolbox

Multisig, timelocks, and hash locks are the primitives every L2 is assembled from: multisig lets a federation collectively hold funds, timelocks let users reclaim coins if a federation vanishes, hash locks enable atomic swaps between layers.

Meanwhile, in the fiat world

The equivalent machinery is contract law — enforced by courts, after the fact, if you can afford them. Here the contract enforces itself: a timelock doesn't need a judge.

Layer 2 · first room

💧 Liquid — the institutional room

Sidechain · est. 2018

A separate blockchain pegged to Bitcoin, built by Blockstream for exchanges, brokers, and OTC desks that need fast, confidential settlement. Think of it as a high-performance settlement layer for bitcoin-native financial institutions.

The peg — how BTC becomes L-BTC and back

Peg-in: lock on L1

Send BTC to a multisig address controlled by the federation. After 102 Bitcoin confirmations (~17 hours), the lock is considered final.

L-BTC issued

Functionaries confirm the lock and issue an equal amount of L-BTC on the sidechain. 1 L-BTC = 1 BTC, always.

Settle at Liquid speed

Blocks every ~1 minute; two-block finality ≈ 2 minutes. Amounts and asset types are hidden by default with confidential transactions.

Peg-out: reclaim on L1

Burn L-BTC; the federation co-signs the release of your BTC. If the federation goes unresponsive, an emergency timelock lets users withdraw anyway.

The trust model, honestly stated

Liquid is run by ~65 functionary companies; peg operations need 11 of 15 active signers. This is not trustless: if 11 of 15 collude, pegged funds can in theory be stolen. But these are known businesses with legal identities, reputations, and hardware security modules — the attack is possible in theory and economically irrational in practice.

The trade: trust a known federation → gain speed, privacy, and assets.

Meanwhile, in the fiat world

Liquid resembles correspondent banking — a club of institutions settling among themselves — with two differences: the trust is quantified and published (exactly 11-of-15; inspect the contract), and there's a unilateral exit: the timelock escape hatch. Your bank's terms of service contain neither.

Layer 2 · second room

🔐 Fedimint — the community room

Federated e-cash

A federated e-cash protocol: a community bank — a local circular economy, a savings group, a family — whose guardians hold the bitcoin but mathematically cannot see who holds what. The trick is blind signatures, invented by David Chaum in the 1980s.

The carbon-paper envelope — how the bank signs what it can't read

Analogy: put a note inside a carbon-paper envelope. A bank official signs the outside — the signature transfers through to the note — but never sees what's written. Open the envelope and you hold a validly signed note the bank cannot link to you.

In Fedimint: you "blind" your token request before sending it to the guardians; they sign it without seeing the serial number; you unblind it into a valid e-cash note. When it's spent, guardians verify their signature but can't connect it to the issuance. Complete unlinkability.

The flow — deposit to spend

Deposit via Lightning

Send sats to the mint over Lightning. Guardians hold the underlying BTC in a threshold multisig — your coins are now in federation custody.

Receive blinded notes

The mint issues denominated e-cash notes (1, 2, 4, 8 sats…). They're bearer instruments — whoever holds the note can spend it. No accounts, no addresses.

Spend privately

Hand notes to anyone; the recipient redeems them for fresh ones. The mint cannot link new notes to old. Fully private inside the federation.

Withdraw via Lightning

Redeem notes back to sats any time — the federation co-signs an outbound Lightning payment and you're back on the open network.

The trust model, honestly stated

Guardians are typically people the community knows. Unlike a bank, they cannot see your balance or transactions — the cryptography guarantees it — but if all guardians collude, they can abscond with the deposited bitcoin. The custody risk is real, and so is the privacy.

The trade: give up self-custody → gain privacy, ease of use, and community resilience.

Meanwhile, in the fiat world

Fedimint is the exact inverse of fiat banking. Your bank sees everything and owes you an IOU; a Fedimint sees nothing and holds real coins for a federation you chose. Similar custody risk — opposite information flow.

Reference

Glossary

Peg-in / peg-out

Locking BTC on Layer 1 to receive an equivalent token on a Layer 2, and burning that token to reclaim the BTC.

Blind signature

A scheme where a signer validly signs a message without seeing its content — how Fedimint guardians issue tokens they can't trace.

Functionary

A Liquid federation member — typically an exchange or broker — running hardware signing modules that co-sign peg operations.

Guardian

A Fedimint federation member holding a share of the multisig key protecting deposits. Usually known to the community.

Confidential transactions

Liquid's cryptographic commitments that hide amounts and asset types from on-chain observers.

E-cash note

A Fedimint bearer token denominated in sats — like physical cash, spendable by whoever holds it. No accounts, no addresses.

The exit isn't a place. It's a property set: rules no committee can change, settlement no court can reverse, and trust you can count on one hand.

Whether these rails carry the world or stay a lifeboat is undecided — but after Chapters I–III, you can evaluate them on the mechanics, not the marketing.

The parallel stack does not require victory in a semantic argument about what “capitalism” means. It only requires rules that cannot be rewritten by the people who benefit from the rewriting. Settlement that is costly to reverse, issuance that is capped by design, and custody that does not depend on institutional permission operate independently of the surrounding moral vocabulary.
