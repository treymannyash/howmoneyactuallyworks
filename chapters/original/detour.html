The School of Salamanca

Three centuries before the Heidelberg pilgrimage, Dominican friars and jurists at a Castilian university confronted the first great inflation of the modern world — and reasoned their way to subjective value, the quantity theory of money, and market-set prices. Then history buried them.

Why this detour exists

Chapter I tells the story of the line that won: Americans trained in 1870s Germany who built a profession around expert management of the economy. This detour is the older line they never learned — one that started from the opposite premise: the exact just price is known to God alone, so no human authority can compute it. The rest is supply and demand.

The setting

An empire drowning in silver

From the 1540s, treasure fleets poured American silver into Castile — and prices climbed for decades, something no living theory could explain. The theologians of Salamanca, obliged to judge the morality of every contract, ended up explaining the mechanics instead.

The doctrine

Five theses, four centuries early

Each thesis below was worked out in scholastic Latin between 1544 and 1605 — and each carries a token: where the same idea is still running, right now.

I.Value is estimation, not substance
Luis Saravia de la Calle, c. 1544 · Diego de Covarrubias, 1554

Saravia de la Calle argued, with some heat, that cost of production plays no part in determining price — a thing is worth what people commonly judge it to be worth, given its usefulness and scarcity. Covarrubias pushed further: an article's value doesn't come from its intrinsic nature but from the estimation in which it is held, which is why the same good fetches different prices in different places.

Token · still running

1544 → 1871 → now. Economics "discovered" this as marginal utility three centuries later (Menger, Jevons, Walras) — and it's why labor-theory and cost-plus pricing keep failing wherever they're tried. Every airline seat priced differently on the same flight is Covarrubias in action.

II.More money, higher prices
Martín de Azpilcueta "Navarrus," 1556

Watching American treasure cross Castile, Azpilcueta produced the first clear statement of the quantity theory of money: the high cost of living was the direct result of the flood of silver. Money, he saw, obeys the same law as everything else — where it is abundant, it is worth less. Jean Bodin made the same claim in 1568 and took the credit for four hundred years; England caught up in 1581.

Token · still running

1556 → 2020 → 2022. The U.S. money supply expanded roughly 40% in two years; inflation then hit a forty-year high. Azpilcueta needed no committee, no model, and no data feed to predict that — only the observation that money is not exempt from scarcity.

III.Money is worth more where it is scarce
Francisco de Vitoria, 1535 · developed by the school through Mercado and García

Vitoria noticed that a thousand ducats paid in Medina bought fewer in Germany when money was scarce there — and rightly so, he judged. His successors built this into a theory of the exchanges: currencies trade at rates reflecting what money actually buys in each place. Grice-Hutchinson called it the school's most original achievement — an anticipation of purchasing-power parity, a doctrine "not usually associated with the sixteenth century."

Token · still running

1535 → today, daily. Purchasing-power parity is now the baseline model of the $7-trillion-a-day FX market — it's the theory inside the Big Mac Index, and the reason currency pegs that fight it (Argentina, repeatedly) eventually snap.

IV.The just price is the market price
The school's consensus · sharpened by Luis de Molina and, later, Juan de Lugo

Medieval doctrine demanded a "just price" — the school asked who could possibly know it. Their answer: absent fraud or monopoly, the just price is the common price formed in the market, because it aggregates every buyer's and seller's estimation. Lugo put the theological ceiling on it: the mathematically exact just price is known to God alone. Everyone else gets the market.

Token · still running

1605 → 1945 → every price control since. Hayek's famous insight — that prices transmit knowledge no planner can gather — is this thesis in modern dress. And every attempt to overrule it, from Diocletian to 1970s price freezes to modern rent caps, has produced the same shortages the friars would have predicted.

V.Trade is a natural right
Francisco de Vitoria, lectures of the 1530s

Asked to judge Spain's claims in the Americas, Vitoria reshaped the old ius gentium — the law of peoples — around a radical premise: peoples, including the conquered, hold natural rights, and among them the right to trade freely with one another. His lectures are recognized as a foundation of international law, transmitted through Grotius.

Token · still running

1535 → 1625 → now. The legal skeleton of the modern trading world — that commerce between nations is a right, not a favor granted by princes — runs through Vitoria. Scholars traced Grotius's debts to the Spaniards as early as 1730.

The disappearance

How it got buried — and dug back up

The school didn't fail — it was forgotten. Its economics lay encased in Latin treatises on moral theology, formatted as Questions, Objections, and Solutions — invisible to a discipline that decided its own story began with Adam Smith in 1776. Spain's decline and the Black Legend did the rest: nobody went looking for the origins of free-market thought in the country of the Inquisition.

The recovery took four centuries and came from the edges of the profession:

1943
José Larraz connects the Spanish writers as a coherent school with a shared monetary doctrine.
1952
Marjorie Grice-Hutchinson, encouraged and advised by F. A. Hayek at the LSE, publishes The School of Salamanca — translating the key texts and naming the three original contributions: subjective value, the quantity theory, and purchasing-power parity.
1954
Joseph Schumpeter's monumental history concludes the scholastics came closer than anyone to founding economics as a science.
1970s–
Murray Rothbard and the Austrian school claim the Salamancans as their true ancestors — "proto-Austrians" — rerouting the family tree of free-market economics from Glasgow to Castile.
The payoff

Two lines, one fork

Hold this detour next to Chapter I and the whole hub snaps into focus. Two traditions answered the same question — who should set economic outcomes? — in opposite ways.

◆ The Salamanca line · 1544
  1. Value lives in human estimation.
  2. Prices therefore carry knowledge no authority holds.
  3. Money itself obeys supply and demand — so it must be kept honest.
  4. The exact just price is God's; the state's job is to police fraud, not to compute prices.
The Heidelberg line · 1872 → Chapter I
  1. Outcomes should be consciously designed.
  2. Trained experts, not markets, should do the designing.
  3. Money is a policy instrument to be managed.
  4. Institutions — associations, journals, textbooks — reproduce the managers.

Chapter I is the story of how the second line built the modern economics profession. This detour is the first line — the road that was open, and not taken.
