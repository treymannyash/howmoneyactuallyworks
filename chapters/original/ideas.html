I. The German Training Ground ›
1870s – 1890s
There were essentially no PhD programs in economics in the United States. Young Americans who wanted serious training had one option: Germany. What they found wasn't just methodology — it was a complete worldview about the relationship between expertise, the state, and society.
1870s
The Pilgrimage to Germany
Between 1870 and 1900, at least 48 Americans who would become professional economists earned doctorates at German universities — Heidelberg, Berlin, Halle, and Göttingen. The attraction: Germany had the most advanced university system in the world. America had almost nothing — no research universities, no economics departments, no PhD programs.
Richard T. Ely → Heidelberg Henry C. Adams → Berlin E.R.A. Seligman → Berlin/Heidelberg Simon Patten → Halle Edward A. Ross → Berlin

"The great error of individualism is to suppose that economic outcomes emerge from impersonal natural forces rather than from social arrangements that can be consciously guided by reason, expertise, and the power of the state."
When was this written?
Richard T. Ely, paraphrasing the Verein position — AEA founding materials, 1885
The language reads as contemporary progressive economics. It was the operating premise of the German Historical School. The argument has never really changed — only the credentials of those making it.
1872
The Verein für Socialpolitik
German economists led by Gustav von Schmoller found the Verein für Socialpolitik — an organization that explicitly rejects Manchestertum (British-style laissez-faire) and argues the state must direct economic life. Schmoller's core claim: that trained academic experts, not market forces, should determine social outcomes. The Americans absorb this model and bring it home wholesale.
Gustav von Schmoller Adolph Wagner Karl Knies Wilhelm Roscher
Ideology
"Central bank independence must be protected precisely because monetary policy is too technically complex to be made through democratic processes."
— Christine Lagarde, IMF Managing Director, Davos, 2016
Schmoller's argument in 1872 was structurally identical: trained academic experts, insulated from popular pressure, must direct economic outcomes. The 21st-century version has better branding.
Historical Context — The Kathedersozialist
The German Historical School was born in Bismarck-era politics. German academics had a direct role in government that British or American scholars did not. The Kathedersozialist ("socialists of the lectern") believed the professor's proper role was to guide the state. These men wrote legislation, sat on royal commissions, and saw themselves as the intellectual vanguard of a managed society. The Americans internalized not just the ideas but the professional identity: the economist as state planner.
1875–85
Karl Knies and the Heidelberg Pipeline
Karl Knies at Heidelberg becomes the single most important mentor to the American generation. He teaches that economic "laws" are not universal but historically contingent — each nation must discover its own path, guided by its educated class. His American students include Richard T. Ely (future AEA founder), Henry Carter Adams (future railroad regulation architect), and Davis Rich Dewey (future AER editor for 30 years).
Karl Knies (mentor) Richard T. Ely Henry Carter Adams Davis Rich Dewey

Embedded Myth #1
"Economics is a value-free science like physics"
The German-trained generation deliberately positioned economics as a "science" to gain authority — but it was never value-free. From the beginning, the founders embedded specific normative commitments (state intervention, expert management, racial hierarchy) inside what they presented as objective findings. The scientific framing was itself a political strategy to insulate their policy preferences from democratic challenge.
Economics has always been entangled with ideology. The original "scientists" chose which questions to ask, which data to collect, and which populations to study — all shaped by prior political commitments. The scientific framing gave their preferences the authority of fact.
1876
Johns Hopkins University Opens
America's first research university, modeled directly on the German system by Daniel Coit Gilman. Prioritizes graduate research and the seminar method over undergraduate teaching. Richard T. Ely gets his first American professorship here in 1881 and immediately begins training the next generation. Johns Hopkins becomes the template that Harvard, Columbia, Chicago, and Wisconsin will replicate.
Daniel Coit Gilman (president) Richard T. Ely (first econ chair)
Institution
1880s
Simon Patten's "Abundance Economics"
Simon Nelson Patten, trained at Halle under Johannes Conrad, returns to the University of Pennsylvania and argues America has entered an "economy of abundance" where old rules of scarcity no longer apply. The state's role is to manage this abundance through expert administration. His framework — that scarcity is a policy choice, not a natural condition — becomes foundational to later Keynesian and welfare-state thinking.
Simon N. Patten → Penn Johannes Conrad (mentor, Halle)
Ideology
Embedded Myth #2
"Minimum wages help the lowest-paid workers"
The original Progressive economists who advocated for minimum wages were explicit: the laws were meant to exclude workers deemed "unemployable" — racial minorities, immigrants, and women — from the labor force entirely. Henry Rogers Seager of Columbia wrote that minimum wages would act as a sorting mechanism, identifying the "unfit" who should be removed from industry. Sidney Webb called it a tool against "economic parasites."
The modern argument that minimum wages are purely about helping low-wage workers strips away the original eugenic design. Whether minimum wages help or hurt today is a legitimate empirical debate — but the policy was not invented for benevolent reasons. Understanding its origin reveals how "reform" and exclusion were intertwined from the start.
Econ 101 standard
"Raising the minimum wage to $15 is simple justice. No one working full time should live in poverty. The evidence is clear: it won't cost jobs."
— Senator Bernie Sanders, Senate floor statement, 2021
The original progressive case for minimum wages also invoked humanitarian language — while the exclusionary mechanism was written into the statute, not the speech. The humanitarian framing is as old as the policy. The eugenic design rarely appears in either version.
II. Building the American Apparatus ›
1885 – 1910
The German-trained generation returns and builds institutions: professional associations, journals, PhD programs, and entire university departments — all constructed in roughly twenty-five years, all controlled by scholars sharing the same German-derived worldview.
1885
American Economic Association Founded at Saratoga Springs
Richard T. Ely drafts the AEA's founding platform — more political manifesto than scholarly charter. It declares: "We regard the state as an agency whose positive assistance is one of the indispensable conditions of human progress." It explicitly rejects laissez-faire as "unsafe in politics and unsound in morals." Of the 50 charter members, the majority trained in Germany. First generation dissenters — including J. Laurence Laughlin of Chicago — are deliberately marginalized.
Richard T. Ely (founder) Henry Carter Adams Edmund J. James J.L. Laughlin (opponent)
InstitutionIdeology
"Economics is not a matter of opinion. There is a scientific consensus on what policies work. Dismissing peer-reviewed economics is no different from denying climate change."
— Paul Krugman, New York Times, November 2012
Ely's 1885 platform also appealed to "science" to silence dissent — specifically against laissez-faire, which he called "unsafe in politics and unsound in morals." The rhetorical move is identical: claim the prestige of science, then define disagreement as ignorance.

1890s
The PhD Factory Turns On
Columbia under E.R.A. Seligman; Wisconsin under Ely (moved from Hopkins in 1892); Harvard under Frank Taussig; Chicago under Laughlin — all build graduate programs. The PhD becomes the essential credential. A generation earlier, public intellectuals on economics were journalists, ministers, businessmen. Now the gates close. German-trained scholars control nearly every credentialing committee.
E.R.A. Seligman → Columbia Richard T. Ely → Wisconsin Frank Taussig → Harvard J.L. Laughlin → Chicago

Historical Context — The Credential Revolution
Henry George, the most widely-read economic thinker in late 19th century America, was a journalist with no degree. His Progress and Poverty (1879) outsold every economics text. The professionalization project was partly a campaign to displace voices like his. Ely explicitly argued only university-trained experts should advise on policy — simultaneously elevating his authority and silencing popular thinkers. Even the Austrian School (Menger, Böhm-Bawerk) was largely ignored until Hayek arrived at LSE in the 1930s.
1890s
Eugenics and Economics Merge
The same economists building the profession simultaneously lead the eugenics movement. Francis A. Walker (MIT president and AEA president) argues immigration degrades American racial "stock." Edward A. Ross coins "race suicide" and campaigns for Asian exclusion. John R. Commons publishes Races and Immigrants in America (1907), ranking racial groups by economic capacity. These are mainstream, peer-reviewed, professionally endorsed positions.
Francis A. Walker (MIT/AEA pres.) Edward A. Ross → "race suicide" John R. Commons Irving Fisher → eugenics leader

Embedded Myth #3
"Immigration depresses wages for native workers"
This argument was developed by Francis A. Walker and Richmond Mayo-Smith in the 1890s as "scientific" justification for racial exclusion. Walker's theory — that immigrant "hordes" displaced native reproduction — was explicitly eugenic. The economic framework was built backward: start with the conclusion (restrict immigration), then construct the model.
Most modern empirical research finds immigration has small or positive net effects on native wages, with distributional effects varying by sector and skill level. The framing of immigration as inherently wage-depressing — as a "law" — traces directly to eugenic pseudoscience, though it has been stripped of its origins.
1904
"The Wisconsin Idea" — Economists as Legislators
John R. Commons, Ely's star student, builds the most consequential professor-to-policy pipeline in American history. Under Governor Robert La Follette, Commons and his students draft Wisconsin's workers' compensation law, public utility regulation, and the framework that becomes the basis for Social Security. His students Edwin Witte (Social Security Act) and Arthur Altmeyer (SSA administrator for 20 years) carry the model nationwide.
John R. Commons (architect) Robert La Follette (governor) Edwin Witte → Social Security Act Arthur Altmeyer → SSA, 20 yrs

1907
Irving Fisher — Economist as Eugenics Crusader
Irving Fisher of Yale — arguably the most brilliant technical economist of his generation — becomes president of the American Eugenics Society. Fisher views "human capital" (his term) as a stock to be managed and eugenic selection as efficiency policy. He advocates forced sterilization, immigration restriction, and Prohibition within a framework of "scientific" resource optimization. His technical contributions (the Fisher equation, debt-deflation) remain valid — but were produced within a worldview treating populations as inputs to optimize.
Irving Fisher → Yale

Embedded Myth #4
"Government experts can allocate resources better than markets"
The German Historical School's core premise — that trained experts can identify optimal social outcomes — was imported wholesale. This wasn't an empirical finding. It was an axiom, absorbed at Heidelberg and embedded in every institution the returnees built. The belief that credentialed economists possess knowledge sufficient to manage complex systems became the default assumption — rarely tested, simply presupposed.
The 20th century produced extensive evidence that centralized planning faces severe knowledge and incentive problems (Hayek's "knowledge problem," public choice theory, the socialist calculation debate). The blanket assumption that experts outperform decentralized decision-making was more faith than science — persisting because it serves the professional interests of the class that holds it.
Econ 101 standard
"Markets have demonstrably failed to deliver on climate, housing, and healthcare. We need coordinated expert-led public investment to address these market failures — the private sector simply cannot do this alone."
— Janet Yellen, Treasury Secretary, Senate Finance Committee testimony, 2021
The German Historical School's core premise, restated for the 21st century: identified market failures justify expert-coordinated intervention. The logic of the Verein für Socialpolitik, 136 years later, delivered from a Senate hearing room instead of a Heidelberg seminar.
Then vs. Now — Expert Authority
THEN
"The public is incompetent to judge economic questions. The science of political economy must be left to those trained in its methods, or policy will be dictated by sentiment and ignorance."
Richard T. Ely, AEA founding address, 1885
NOW
"We need to listen to the economists. This is a matter of empirical science. When voters reject the expert consensus, the results are predictably catastrophic."
Larry Summers, interview, The Atlantic, 2016
Same argument. Same structure. Same appeal to expertise as a bar to democratic input. 130 years apart.

1910 – 1930
The infrastructure is complete. What happens next is consolidation: controlling who publishes, who gets hired, who advises government — and who gets defined out of the profession entirely.
1911
American Economic Review Launches
Davis Rich Dewey — Knies' student from Heidelberg — becomes editor and controls the journal for 30 years. Publication in the AER becomes essential for tenure. The journal enforces methodological and ideological norms: empirical work framed within interventionist assumptions is favored. Classical liberal perspectives are systematically underrepresented.
Davis Rich Dewey (editor, 30 yrs)

E.R.A. Seligman of Columbia is the chief academic architect of the progressive income tax (16th Amendment). The Federal Reserve's design draws on debates dominated by the German-trained generation. J. Embedded Myth #5
"The Federal Reserve is a neutral, technocratic institution"
The Fed was presented as a purely technical solution to banking panics. But its creation implemented the German model: expert management of the economy through a centralized institution insulated from democratic accountability. The assumption that monetary policy should be managed by credentialed experts rather than constrained by rules (like the gold standard) was the Verein worldview applied to American banking.
The Fed is a political institution that makes distributional choices — who benefits from easy money, who bears inflation costs, which sectors get support during crises. Framing these as "technical" decisions obscures the reality that monetary policy creates winners and losers, and the experts making those calls have their own class interests and biases.
Still in Fed models
"The Federal Reserve's independence is sacrosanct. Political interference with monetary policy decisions would be catastrophic for the credibility of our institutions and ultimately for ordinary Americans."
— Jerome Powell, Fed Chair, press conference remarks, July 2019
Every Fed chair since Marriner Eccles has used nearly identical language. The "independence" framing originated with the same Verein model that treated expert insulation as self-evidently good — the question of what the Fed's independence is for has never been satisfactorily answered.
1917–24
Immigration Restriction Becomes Law
Three decades of economic "science" culminate in law. The Immigration Act of 1917 and Johnson-Reed Act of 1924 (national origins quotas) are directly informed by AEA members' arguments. The Dillingham Commission (1907–11) relied on work by Jeremiah Jenks of Cornell. Quotas are designed to reduce Southern/Eastern European immigration and bar Asians entirely — justified by economic arguments about wage depression and "assimilability" developed inside the academy.
Jeremiah Jenks → Dillingham Commission Francis A. Walker (intellectual architect)

IV. The Keynesian Takeover ›
1930 – 1946
The Depression doesn't create the interventionist apparatus — it activates it. The institutions built by the German-trained generation are already in place. Keynes provides the theoretical superstructure for a building whose foundation was poured forty years earlier.
1933
The New Deal Brain Trust
FDR's advisors are products of the German-influenced system. Rexford Tugwell (Columbia, student of Wesley Clair Mitchell) explicitly admires Mussolini's corporatist planning. Adolf Berle (Columbia) co-architects the New Deal. Commons' student Edwin Witte chairs the committee drafting the Social Security Act (1935). Another Commons student, Arthur Altmeyer, runs the SSA for its first two decades. The "Wisconsin Idea" goes national.
Rexford Tugwell → Brain Trust Adolf Berle → Brain Trust Edwin Witte → Social Security Arthur Altmeyer → SSA Wesley C. Mitchell → NBER founder

Embedded Myth #6
"FDR's New Deal ended the Great Depression"
Perhaps the most consequential economic myth in American public life, cemented by Keynesian economists with professional incentives to validate the interventionist model. Unemployment remained above 14% through 1940. What ended mass unemployment was WWII military mobilization. The New Deal provided relief and institutional reform, but as economic recovery the evidence is far weaker than the textbook narrative.
Robert Higgs argued the New Deal prolonged the Depression through regime uncertainty. Christina Romer showed monetary policy, not fiscal, drove pre-war recovery. The truth is genuinely contested — but the "FDR saved the economy" story was promoted by economists whose careers depended on the interventionist model being vindicated.
1936
Keynes Publishes The General Theory
John Maynard Keynes provides the theoretical architecture: economies can get stuck in underemployment; aggregate demand matters; government spending fills the gap; saving can be a "paradox." Critically, he doesn't have to convince American economists that government should act — they already believe that. What he provides is a model — equations and diagrams that can be taught and published. He transforms the German-derived interventionist instinct into something that looks like science.
John Maynard Keynes
Ideology
Embedded Myth #7
"The Paradox of Thrift — saving is bad for the economy"
Keynes argued that if everyone saves simultaneously, total demand falls and everyone ends up poorer. Samuelson enshrined this as a core principle in his textbook. For decades, the "paradox" was taught as fundamental insight justifying government spending to compensate for "excessive" saving.
The paradox assumes savings are stuffed in mattresses rather than channeled through financial markets into investment. In reality, savings fund capital formation — the mechanism creating future productive capacity. The "paradox" only works under very specific assumptions (liquidity trap, zero bound) that were generalized into a universal principle. The practical effect: making consumption seem virtuous and saving harmful — a convenient argument for governments that wanted to spend.
Déjà vu
Search "paradox of thrift" in your intro macro textbook — it's there. Typically appears in the Keynesian expenditure model or aggregate demand chapters. Mankiw covers it; so does every AP Macro prep book. It's usually presented as a settled insight, not an assumption-dependent edge case.

Embedded Myth #8
"Government spending multiplies through the economy"
The "multiplier" — $1 of government spending generates more than $1 of activity — became Keynesian gospel via Richard Kahn's 1931 paper. Samuelson's textbook taught students to calculate it as 1/(1-MPC). It became the core justification for deficit spending.
Decades of research show the multiplier is highly context-dependent and often below 1.0 — meaning government spending can reduce total activity by crowding out private investment. Robert Barro found multipliers near 0.8 for non-defense spending. Wartime estimates aren't generalizable to peacetime. The textbook version systematically overstates it.
Déjà vu
You almost certainly calculated this in class: Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC). That formula is in every intro macro textbook and on the AP Macro exam. It's usually presented as a clean, reliable tool — the empirical murkiness isn't mentioned.

V. The Textbook Lock-In ›
1948 – 1970
A single textbook captures the consensus and transmits it to millions. The Keynesian framework stops being debatable and becomes the way economics is taught — the mental operating system installed in every Econ 101 student. If you took economics in high school or college, this is the era that shaped what you learned. Samuelson's 1948 synthesis still runs underneath Mankiw's Principles of Economics (the most widely assigned college textbook today), Krugman & Wells, McConnell, Brue & Flynn (dominant in AP courses), and the free OpenStax Principles of Economics. The structure — circular flow, AD/AS, fiscal multiplier, Phillips curve, GDP accounting — was fixed here.
1948
Samuelson's Economics — The Textbook That Defined a Discipline
Paul Samuelson's textbook: 19 editions, 4M+ copies, 40+ languages. He synthesizes Keynesian macro with neoclassical micro into the "neoclassical synthesis" — Keynesian demand management as the macro framework, marginalist analysis as micro, presented as complementary parts of unified science. This synthesis is Samuelson's invention, not Keynes's — and it becomes the default mental model for two generations.
Paul Samuelson → MIT
TextbookInstitution
Déjà vu — this is the origin
Every intro economics course you've taken descends directly from this synthesis. Today's standard — Mankiw's Principles of Economics (now in its 9th edition) — is structurally the same book, updated. If you took AP Economics, the circular flow diagram, the spending multiplier, the Phillips curve tradeoff, and the AD-AS model all trace back here.

Embedded Myth #9
"Recessions are caused by insufficient aggregate demand"
Samuelson's textbook teaches this as the explanation: people aren't spending enough, output falls, unemployment rises. Solution: government fills the "demand gap." The framework treats recessions as one type of problem with one type of solution — so thoroughly drilled that many economists couldn't conceive alternatives.
Recessions have multiple causes — malinvestment from credit expansion (Austrian theory), real shocks (RBC theory), financial fragility (Minsky), policy uncertainty. "Demand deficiency" is one lens, not the only one. Its textbook dominance crowded out competing explanations for decades, leaving economists poorly equipped for crises like 2008 that didn't fit the simple demand-gap story.
Déjà vu — the AD-AS diagram
The aggregate demand / aggregate supply model — the diagram with the downward-sloping AD curve shifting left during recessions — is the visual centerpiece of intro macro. Every high school AP course and college Econ 101 section builds around it. It encodes this exact assumption: recessions are demand shortfalls, cured by shifting AD rightward.

Embedded Myth #10
"Government can trade inflation for lower unemployment"
For a decade, this was the master equation. Policymakers believed they could permanently buy lower unemployment by accepting more inflation. Taught in every textbook, used by every central bank, underlying the Kennedy-Johnson economic program. Walter Heller used it to design the 1964 tax cut.
Milton Friedman (1967) and Edmund Phelps (1968) predicted the tradeoff was temporary: once inflation expectations adjusted, you'd get the same unemployment with higher inflation. The 1970s proved them exactly right. Stagflation should have killed the Phillips Curve as a policy tool. Instead, it was patched, modified, and quietly retained in weaker form. Most undergrads still learn a version.
Déjà vu
If you took AP Macro or Econ 101, you were tested on the short-run vs. long-run Phillips Curve distinction — the "patch" added after stagflation. The long-run curve is vertical (Friedman was right), but the short-run version is still taught as a usable policy tool. The same concept that failed in the 1970s lives on in adapted form in Mankiw, Krugman & Wells, McConnell & Brue, and every AP prep book.

VI. The Monetarist Counter-Revolution ›
1970 – 1990
Stagflation breaks the Keynesian consensus — but not the infrastructure. Friedman and Chicago offer alternatives, but work within the same apparatus the German-trained generation built. The critique changes what's taught but not how the profession is organized.
1970–75
Stagflation Breaks the Model
Simultaneous high inflation (12% by 1974) and high unemployment — impossible under Phillips Curve logic. Arthur Burns at the Fed tries demand management and makes inflation worse. Nixon imposes wage and price controls in 1971 — the ultimate Keynesian intervention — and they fail catastrophically.
Arthur Burns → Fed chair Richard Nixon → wage/price controls

1976
Friedman Wins the Nobel — Chicago Ascends
Milton Friedman and the Chicago School — George Stigler (regulatory capture), Gary Becker (human capital), Robert Lucas (rational expectations) — offer an alternative: monetarism, skepticism of intervention, rules over discretion. But crucially, they operate within the same structure: AEA, top journals, PhD pipelines. Friedman doesn't destroy the apparatus — he captures parts of it.
Milton Friedman → Chicago George Stigler Robert Lucas Gary Becker

Historical Context — The Counter-Revolution's Limits
Friedman's monetarism still assumed credentialed experts should manage the money supply through a central bank — he just wanted rules rather than discretion. The deeper German inheritance — that the economy should be managed by professionals through centralized institutions — was never challenged. Even the "free market" 1980s was implemented by economists working through the same Fed, CEA, and academic pipeline.
1979
Volcker's Shock — Monetarism in Practice
Paul Volcker raises the federal funds rate to over 20% to crush inflation. It works — inflation falls from 13% to 3% — but causes the worst recession since the Depression (unemployment peaks at 10.8%). First major policy action justified by monetarist rather than Keynesian logic. Demonstrates that central bank decisions have enormous distributional consequences.
Paul Volcker → Fed chair
Policy
Embedded Myth #11
"Deficits don't matter" / "We owe it to ourselves"
A persistent Keynesian argument: government debt isn't a problem because it's mostly owed domestically. Abba Lerner's "functional finance" (1943) argued deficits should be judged only by effects on employment, not size. This evolved into casual dismissal of debt concerns characterizing Keynesian policy advice for decades. Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) is the latest version.
Government debt transfers from future taxpayers to present bondholders — "we" don't owe it to "ourselves" in any meaningful sense. Debt service consumes increasing budget shares, crowds out other spending, and constrains future policy. The casual dismissal of fiscal constraints — embedded since Lerner, amplified through Samuelson's textbook — has enabled systematically reckless fiscal policy.
VII. The Legacy — What Persists ›
1990 – Present
Intellectual content evolves — "New Keynesian" models replace old ones, behavioral economics challenges rational actors. But the institutional architecture the German-trained generation built between 1876 and 1910 remains largely intact.
Today
The Architecture Endures
The AEA still controls the profession's premier journal and meeting. The PhD pipeline from ~10 elite departments still produces most influential economists. The CEA still advises every president. The Fed still manages money through expert discretion. Textbooks — now Mankiw's instead of Samuelson's, but structurally similar — still teach Keynesian demand management as default macro. The architecture Ely, Seligman, and Commons built in the 1880s–1900s is the skeleton American economics still walks on.
N.G. Mankiw → current textbook Fed → Bernanke, Yellen, Powell
Institution
Embedded Myth #12
"GDP growth = economic health"
GDP — developed by Simon Kuznets in the 1930s — was designed as a wartime production metric. Kuznets himself warned: "the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income." But the Keynesian framework, built around managing aggregate output, made GDP the master metric. Every policy debate, election, and textbook chapter revolves around it.
GDP doesn't measure wealth distribution, environmental costs, household production, leisure, or health. A hurricane that destroys homes and triggers rebuilding increases GDP. A parent leaving work to raise children decreases it. The metric's centrality reflects Keynesian emphasis on aggregate demand, not inherent superiority. We built an entire policy apparatus around optimizing a number its inventor told us not to optimize.
Déjà vu — chapter one of every textbook
GDP is introduced in the opening chapters of every economics course at every level. High school students learn the expenditure formula GDP = C + I + G + NX as one of the first things in the class. College courses add real vs. nominal, deflators, and per-capita adjustments — but the framing of GDP as the primary measure of economic health is inherited, not defended. The formula appears in Mankiw, Krugman & Wells, McConnell & Brue, OpenStax, and every AP prep book — usually before the chapter that briefly notes its limitations.

The 2008 Crisis — The System Stress-Tests Itself
The financial crisis exposes failures at every level. Dominant macro models (DSGE) don't include financial sectors. The Fed doesn't see it coming. The "efficient markets hypothesis" fails catastrophically. Response: massive Keynesian stimulus (TARP, ARRA) justified by multiplier estimates that prove too optimistic. Larry Summers revives Hansen's 1938 "secular stagnation" to explain slow recovery. The cycle completes: Hansen's theory, filtered through Samuelson's textbook, returns as 21st-century policy.

Ideology
The Final Irony
The German-trained generation built American economics to be a tool of reform. 150 years later, the system they built has produced a profession that is remarkably insular, intellectually homogeneous, and resistant to outside critique. The gatekeeping designed to exclude "unscientific" voices now excludes heterodox economists, non-Western perspectives, and practical business knowledge. The pipeline from elite PhD to elite department to policy role remains extraordinarily narrow. The architecture endures — but whether it serves the public or merely reproduces itself is the question the profession has never adequately answered.

The Throughline

The German Historical School economists didn't just bring ideas to America — they built the institutions that reproduced those ideas: the PhD program, the professional association, the policy advisory role, the introductory textbook. Keynes didn't create a new system; he provided the most powerful theory for a system already built and waiting. Each generation trained the next within the same infrastructure, and at every stage, certain assumptions hardened into "laws" taught as settled science: that experts can manage economies, that government spending multiplies wealth, that saving is suspect, that GDP equals progress. These weren't empirical discoveries — they were axioms embedded by the founders and transmitted through the pipeline they built. The myths persist because the institutions that propagate them were designed, from the beginning, to be self-reinforcing.

The same intellectual tradition that rejected universal economic laws also proved useful for redefining terms. Once “capitalism” could be stretched to cover both relatively open markets and heavily managed mixed economies, almost any outcome could be attributed to it. The word became a portable explanation rather than a precise description of institutions. That semantic flexibility later proved valuable to those defending discretionary monetary and legal systems.
